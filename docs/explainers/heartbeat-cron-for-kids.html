🏠 The little robot who keeps the house running

Heartbeat, checklist, and cron routines — explained as if you were five.

The house is your company. A little robot lives in it, and its whole job is to keep the house running. But here's the thing about this robot: it can't think all the time. It sleeps. Then it wakes up, does a quick round, and goes back to sleep. Over and over, forever.

1 The heartbeat = the robot waking up

Every 5 minutes an alarm goes beep. The robot wakes up. 💓

That beep is the heartbeat. One beep = one wake-up = the robot opens its eyes, does a round of chores, and goes back to sleep. If the alarm ever stopped, the robot would sleep forever and nothing would ever get done. The beep is what makes the house alive instead of a frozen room full of stuff.

One beep is called a tick. Beep = tick = “robot does one round.”

2 The checklist = what it does each time it wakes

The robot is not clever. It doesn't improvise. Every time it wakes up it reads the same little checklist and does the items in the same order:

🤖 My checklist (I do this EVERY beep):
   1. Did anyone fall down and get stuck? → help them up      (recover)
   2. Is it time for any "every-day chores"? → start them     (cron)
   3. Did anyone leave a note "wake me at 3pm"? → wake them    (monitors)
   4. Is anyone ready to work? → tell them to go work          (dispatch)

That's the checklist (in the code it's called the tick). Same 4 things, same order, every beep. Boring = reliable.

3 A cron routine = a sticky-note on the fridge

Item 2 of the checklist (“every-day chores”) is where cron routines come in. A cron routine is just a sticky-note on the fridge:

🟨 “Water the plants every morning at 9am.”

The robot doesn't water the plants because it's smart. It waters them because every time it wakes up, item 2 says “check the fridge — is any chore due right now?” At 9am one morning it wakes, reads the note, sees “yep, 9am,” and starts the plant-watering job.

  • The sticky-note = the cron routine (the standing instruction).
  • The “9am every morning” = the cron (the schedule — when).
  • The robot reading the notes on its round = the scheduler's cron step.

The one confusing bit, made simple

✅ The robot already checks the fridge for sticky-notes every round — that part works.

❌ But there's no pen in the house to write a sticky-note with. So the fridge is always empty, and the robot keeps checking nothing.

M4 is just: give the house a pen 🖊️ so you can finally write a note (“water the plants at 9am”). The moment you write one, the robot — who's been checking the empty fridge this whole time — starts doing it. That “pen” is the add_routine verb.

4 The round, one chore at a time

Remember the robot's checklist has 4 items, and it always does them in this exact order: recover → cron → monitors → dispatch. Here's what each one really means, in house words.

🩹 1. Pick up anyone who fell down recover

Imagine someone was carrying a tall pile of laundry, tripped, and is now stuck on the floor under it. Before starting anything new, the robot looks around: did anyone get stuck mid-chore? If so, it helps them up and puts their half-done work back where everyone can see it. You do this first — because if you start new chores while someone's stuck under the laundry, you'll just trip over them and make a bigger mess.

🤖 In robot words: a worker was doing a job but its “beat” froze or died. Recover makes that lost work visible again so it isn't forgotten forever.

🗒️ 2. Do the chores that are due now cron

Now the robot reads the fridge sticky-notes (the cron routines). Is any one of them due right this minute? If it's 9am and the note says “water the plants every 9am,” the robot starts that chore. Notes that aren't due yet are left alone for a later round.

🤖 In robot words: check every routine's schedule; for each one that's due, fire it — which creates a fresh task. (This is the step that was always checking an empty fridge until we add a pen!)

⏰ 3. Wake the people who asked to be woken monitors

Earlier, someone said: “I can't frost the cake until it finishes baking — come poke me in 30 minutes.” That's a personal alarm they set for themselves. The robot checks: has it been 30 minutes yet? If yes, it goes and pokes them so they can carry on.

🤖 In robot words: a task parked itself with “wake me later.” A monitor is that snooze alarm. How is this different from cron? Cron is a repeating note on the fridge for everyone (“every 9am, forever”). A monitor is a one-time “wake me at this moment” that a single worker left for itself.

🛠️ 4. Send everyone who's ready off to work dispatch

Finally, the robot looks around the tidied-up house: who is awake, has a job to do, and has nothing blocking them? It taps each of them on the shoulder — “you, go do your job now” — and they get to work. This is the only step where the actual work happens; the first three were all getting-ready.

🤖 In robot words: find every task that's eligible right now and start a beat for it — the real call where an employee actually does something.

🧠 Why this exact order? (it's not random)

  1. Recover first — pick up the fallen person before anything, or you'll keep tripping over them.
  2. Cron second — start the timed chores so a 9am job never gets missed.
  3. Monitors third — poke awake anyone whose snooze alarm just went off.
  4. Dispatch last — only now send people to work, because steps 1–3 may have just freed a stuck worker or created brand-new jobs. Doing “go work” last means the robot acts on the freshest, tidiest picture of the house.
