# OKRs and metrics

A goal you can't measure is a hope. Every material call needs a number that tells you, early, whether
it's working — and a guardrail number that tells you when to stop. Good metrics change behaviour;
vanity metrics only decorate a slide.

## When NOT to use this
- The beat is a pure narrative decision with no measurable outcome. Don't force a fake metric.

## The method
1. **Pick ONE north-star.** The single metric that best proxies the outcome you actually want (e.g.
   profit-per-cost, not raw revenue). One, not five.
2. **Define leading and lagging indicators.** The lagging metric confirms success late; the leading one
   moves early and lets you steer. Name both.
3. **Set a concrete target.** A number and a date: "profit-per-cost ≥ 0.40 by end of Q3." "Improve
   efficiency" is not a target.
4. **Add a guardrail metric.** The thing that must NOT get worse while you chase the target (e.g. don't
   grow profit by starving quality). A target without a guardrail invites gaming.
5. **Make it verifiable and honest.** Prefer metrics computed from ground truth over self-reported
   ones; a number that can be tuned to the bar isn't a check.

## Rules
- **One north-star,** relentlessly. Subordinate metrics support it; they don't compete.
- **Leading + lagging.** Steer on the leading indicator; confirm on the lagging one.
- **Target = number + date.** Vague direction is not a target.
- **Guardrail every target,** so you don't win the metric and lose the business.

## Common failure modes
- Vanity metrics that go up without the business improving.
- Targets with no date, or no guardrail (gamed by starving something else).
- Too many "key" metrics — none of them actually key.
